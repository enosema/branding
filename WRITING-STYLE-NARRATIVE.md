# Narrative writing style

This document defines the narrative writing style of the Enosema
Foundation ("ES"): the prose of www.enosema.org, blog posts, product
and project summaries, release notes and public announcements. It is
the narrative companion of `WRITING-STYLE-STANDARDS.md`, which defines the
standards writing style of ES publications; the two styles share the
sentence rules and disagree only where their readers differ — a
standard is consulted by a reviewer, the website is read by a visitor.

The style is observed in the corpora the Foundation maintains: the
editorial prose of www.enosema.org and the announcements of its
standards program. Two registers apply: the explanation register
governs descriptive pages and posts, and the announcement register
governs news.

## Part I — the sentence

1. **Every sentence is a complete sentence.** Each sentence carries a
   subject and a verb and stands on its own. Fragments used as style —
   headline clips, tag lines, one-word paragraphs — are not used in
   prose. This rule admits no exception, because a fragment omits the
   agent of an action and therefore omits responsibility for it. The
   brand tagline "Making meaning meaningful." is a brand mark, not a
   sentence, and is exempt where it appears as a mark.

2. **A sentence may be long, and length is not a fault.** Information
   is carried in subordinate clauses introduced by "that", "which",
   "allowing" and "so that", so that one sentence states a fact
   together with its consequence. The model of the construction:

   > The Enosema Foundation maintains the concept register at
   > www.enosema.org/concept-register/, allowing terminology management
   > systems to validate the relation types of EFS 2510 directly
   > against the registered vocabulary instead of copying it.

3. **A term is written in full at first use, followed by its
   abbreviation in parentheses; the abbreviation is used thereafter.**
   For example, "the Enosema Standards Registry (ESR)" at first use,
   "ESR" from that point onward. A standard identifier is cited as
   published: "EFS 2510:2026".

4. **Claims are grounded in named entities, dates and numbers.** An
   announcement states what was published, with which number, in which
   category, and where it can be read. Adjectives do not carry the
   argument; facts do. "The new standard defines…" is completed by the
   number and the title in the same sentence.

5. **A referenced entity is introduced before it is relied upon.**
   When a post depends on a standard, a register or a process, the
   following sentence defines that entity, or links the page that
   defines it.

6. **Attributed, fully-sentenced statements carry the human voice.**
   Where a motivation or a judgement appears in an announcement, it
   appears inside a complete quotation attributed to a named role:
   a quotation of the form "said the chair" without the sentence that
   carries the content is not used.

## Part II — the register

7. **Third person and declarative.** Announcements are reported in the
   present perfect ("has published", "has been verified"); explanations
   are written in the present tense. The reader is not addressed in the
   second person in descriptive prose, imperatives are reserved for
   instructions ("Subscribe", "Read the standard"), and rhetorical
   questions are not used.

8. **The artifact is an admissible subject.** Explanations are written
   with the document or the register as the subject in the
   declarative:

   > EFS 2522:2026 specifies what a good designation is, what a good
   > definition is, and what a complete entry contains, and it attaches
   > a scoring model to those requirements.

9. **The introduction establishes the problem before the solution.**
   An explanatory post first states the challenge in flowing
   declarative paragraphs, and only then introduces the document,
   register or feature that answers it.

10. **Formal enumerations are grammatically parallel.** A list
    introduced by "the standard specifies:" continues with clauses that
    share one grammatical structure.

11. **Boundaries are stated explicitly.** What a release does not
    include, or what a document does not cover, is written as a
    sentence and is not implied by omission.

12. **The vocabulary is formal and precise.** Preferred constructions
    include "in accordance with", "is applicable for", "specifies
    requirements for" and "is available at". Contractions are not used
    in descriptive prose. The em dash marks a parenthetical and is
    never a dramatic pause; no sentence ends on an ellipsis.

## Part III — the standards connection

13. **Narrative prose describes; it does not republish.** A blog post
    about a standard summarizes, quotes within licence, and links to
    the published document at the Enosema Standards Registry. The
    normative text itself is not copied into narrative pages.

14. **Every claim about a document is checkable at the link.** Where a
    post states that a standard specifies something, the sentence
    carries the link to the document page, so that the reader can
    verify the claim against the published text.

15. **Numbers of the program are stated consistently.** The count of
    published documents, their categories and their identifiers follow
    the registry: "seven standards", "five directives", "one guide",
    cited by identifier — not "a dozen documents".

## Part IV — what this style excludes

- Fragments written for rhythm: "Quality has a number now.", "13
  documents. One register."
- Teaser lines, exclamations, and vocabulary that anticipates a
  marketing register rather than an academic one.
- Coined compound labels in place of sentences: "quality scorecards
  for terms" as a headline; written out instead as a sentence that
  names the mechanism.
- Em dashes as pauses; ellipses; one-word paragraphs; implied
  subjects.

## A worked example

Before (fragments, teaser register):

> Quality has a number now. EFS 2522 drops. 21 criteria. Grade your
> terms. Ship better definitions. 🔥

After (house style):

> The Enosema Foundation has published EFS 2522:2026, Concept entry
> quality: Guidelines, requirements and scoring. The standard
> specifies 21 scoring criteria for concept entries, organized into
> five dimension groups — designation, definition, completeness,
> clarity and consistency — and defines grade bands from A to D that
> a term reviewer applies to a proposed entry. The full text is
> available at the Enosema Standards Registry, where the scoring model
> can be verified against the worked examples in its annexes.
